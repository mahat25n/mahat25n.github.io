Willamette: A River’s Memory

Seen from above, the Willamette floodplain contains curved traces that continue away from the present river. Some follow tight bends. Others form broad arcs across fields and low ground. Their shapes are easier to see when small changes in elevation receive as much attention as the current river. This map looks closely at one reach near the Santiam confluence in Oregon, where the floodplain holds traces of more than one course.

The data behind the map

The elevation surface comes from the Oregon Department of Geology and Mineral Industries. I used its Willamette Valley North bare-earth terrain layer with the provider’s 2023 project label. The bare-earth model is designed to represent the ground surface with vegetation and structures removed as far as the processing permits. It makes shallow features easier to distinguish where trees or other cover would obscure them in an aerial photograph. The source documents the project label. The exact flight dates were not established for this map. DOGAMI elevation mosaic ↗

I used QGIS to combine the elevation colours and hillshade in one map frame. The colour ramp spans 49 to 64 metres of absolute elevation, with endpoint colours applied outside that range. It does not show height above the river. A low surface in one part of the map can have the same colour as a surface elsewhere even when the local relationship to the river differs. The narrow range helps reveal the shallow arcs, depressions and raised surfaces that a regional elevation map would compress into a much smaller share of its palette.

How a river leaves traces

A river can reshape a bend as it erodes the outer bank and deposits sediment on the inside. If a bend is eventually cut off, part of its former course can remain as a curved low area after the main channel has shifted. Repeated changes produce a floodplain with traces from different periods. DOGAMI’s historical-channel poster documents this broader pattern on the Willamette and shows the Santiam entering the river in the same part of the valley. DOGAMI’s historical-channel map ↗

The terrain gives readers clues to former channels. The map does not date individual traces or reconstruct the order in which they formed. Some edges in the image follow roads, banks or fields. A convincing interpretation requires looking at how a feature curves and connects to surrounding ground, then comparing it with historical maps, photographs or field evidence. The shaded surface is a way to locate those questions.

Why small differences in height matter

On flat ground, a shallow depression or raised bank can help determine where water spreads when the river rises. The same changes in height can matter for wetland restoration, where the connection between a low area and the active channel affects how often the area receives water. A flood assessment would need discharge, water levels and information about structures that obstruct or redirect flow. The terrain alone gives part of that picture. Its contribution is to show how much variation lies within a floodplain that might otherwise look uniform.

The single reach is a deliberate choice for the Borgesian theme. Increasing the geographic extent would provide context for the river’s entire basin, but the narrow curves would become difficult to see. Giving most of the page to this small section puts the individual bends and shallow traces at the scale where a reader can follow them across a field.
