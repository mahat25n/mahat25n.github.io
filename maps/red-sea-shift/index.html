The Red Sea shift

A shipping disruption changes the use of a network, not simply the number of vessels on the water. Avoiding one passage can increase traffic elsewhere, lengthen journeys and alter the amount of vessel capacity required to carry a given cargo. This animation makes that spatial adjustment visible.

The data behind the map

The underlying observations are daily crossings by the container, dry bulk, general cargo, ro-ro and tanker categories from IMF PortWatch and the UN Global Platform. The comparison uses 1–18 November 2023 as its baseline and the February 2024 mean as its final view. Intermediate dates show trailing seven-day means. Red and orange represent declines relative to the baseline, blue small changes, and turquoise and lime increases.

The cost of keeping trade moving

Python combines the count series with schematic maritime corridors on an Equal Earth map. Route width follows traffic relative to each passage’s own baseline. The moving strokes illustrate change; they are not individual AIS ship tracks. Crossings at different monitoring points cannot be added and interpreted as a count of unique vessels, because one vessel can pass several points. Widths are normalised within each passage, so they cannot be compared as absolute traffic volumes across passages.

The economic mechanism is the use of more time and resources to maintain a connection. Longer journeys can affect vessel availability, fuel requirements and firms’ inventory decisions. The map does not estimate any of those costs, identify the cargo aboard the vessels or prove that the same ships moved from the Red Sea to the Cape. It documents a change in observed crossings and locates the adjustment that a fuller transport-cost analysis would need to explain.
