The routes that connect the world

Trade between countries takes place through a much narrower set of physical routes than a conventional political map suggests. Shipping corridors connect producers and consumers, but they also concentrate exposure. A passage that reduces transport distance in ordinary times can become a source of delay when access is disrupted.

Reading the map

This map uses the World Bank and IMF Global Shipping Traffic Density dataset. Brighter lines indicate greater recorded traffic density on a logarithmic scale. The world view uses the Equal Earth projection, while the regional inset uses Lambert Conformal Conic to examine the Red Sea, Gulf of Aden and Strait of Hormuz. The inset makes the geography of constrained passages easier to read.

The economic interpretation

Traffic density is not the same as the value of trade. Ships differ in size, cargo and economic purpose, so a bright route cannot be read as a ranking of export earnings. Nor does this static image show how a disruption changed traffic. It describes the network against which a change would need to be measured.

The economic question is how much flexibility that network offers. A longer route uses vessel time as well as fuel. For firms, delays also change inventory requirements and the time between paying for inputs and receiving revenue. Those are mechanisms to examine, not costs estimated by this map. Understanding the alternatives to a busy corridor is therefore as important as identifying the corridor itself.
