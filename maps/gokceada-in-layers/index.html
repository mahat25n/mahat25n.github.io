Gökçeada in layers

Viewed from above, a road, a stream and the edge of a field can all look like lines on the same surface. I separated six kinds of features on Gökçeada and rotated the island to show where each layer sits in relation to the others. The outline stays in view as the camera turns, giving the viewer a reference for comparing the layers.

The data behind the map

In QGIS, I prepared six OpenStreetMap layers for the island. They show roads, rivers and streams, buildings, beaches, forest and scrub, and farmland and orchards. I used Mapzen Terrain Tiles as the elevation source and brought the layers into Blender for the animation. The relief shows the shape of the terrain. The gaps between the coloured layers are part of the design.

How to read the island

Start with the coast, then follow a road inland. Buildings make it possible to see where the road joins a settlement, while the land-cover layers show where the surrounding land is mapped as farmland or vegetation. The streams provide a second network to compare with the roads. As the view rotates, lines that overlap from one direction come apart and become easier to follow.

At island scale, each layer shows a different kind of connection. Roads link buildings and settlements. Waterways connect parts of the terrain as they drain toward lower ground. Beaches mark particular stretches of the coast. The animation shows the features represented in the source data. Where a layer is sparse, the image reflects the available mapping as well as the geography.
