Global Airline Connections

Air travel changes which places are close in practice. Two cities separated by an ocean may have a direct connection, while two places much nearer on the ground may require a transfer. The pattern is visible when airport pairs are drawn on the same globe. Short routes form dense regional networks. Longer routes connect some of those networks across oceans and continents. Each line in this map represents a direct connection documented in the historical OpenFlights archive.

The data behind the map

I used the OpenFlights route archive, whose updates stopped in June 2014. The airport endpoints come from a later reference snapshot, joined to the historical records using airport identifiers. I kept zero-stop route records with identifiable endpoints and combined records that referred to the same unordered airport pair. A route listed in both directions appears as one connection. The same is true if several airlines listed it or a code-share created additional records. This produced 18,858 distinct pairs. OpenFlights route and airport documentation ↗

In QGIS, I drew each connection as a geodesic between the two airport coordinates. The map uses an Equal Earth projection, with routes split where they cross the date line. That prevents a line from stretching across the frame when its endpoints sit on opposite sides of the map edge. The colours group connections by distance: under 1,500 km, from 1,500 km to under 6,000 km, and 6,000 km or more. The drawn curves show endpoint relationships. Aircraft may fly different paths because of airspace, weather or operational decisions.

How hubs change a journey

Dense concentrations over Europe, North America and parts of East Asia show where many documented connections overlap. The transatlantic arcs reveal links between two large regional networks. An airport with a broad set of connections can bring several smaller markets within reach of each other through a transfer. For a traveller, that can create an itinerary between cities that cannot support a direct service. It can also add waiting time and expose the journey to missed connections.

One connection between two airports has the same status in the archive whether the service was occasional or frequent. The brightness of a dense area comes from multiple lines overlapping and the way they are drawn. It does not measure passenger traffic. A dark area can reflect sparse service, incomplete archive coverage or both. At world scale, the map is best read as a view of the network’s broad shape. It is difficult to trace individual routes across the densest hubs.

What a historical network can tell us

The June 2014 archive provides a dated record of where direct links were documented. It gives readers a baseline for thinking about which regions were connected and where long routes met regional networks. Measuring how those patterns changed would require a comparable archive from another date and consistent coverage rules. Measuring how readily people could use those links would also require service frequency, fares and transfer times. The distinction matters because the existence of a route and the convenience of an itinerary answer different questions about access.

The unit of observation also matters when comparing places. A metropolitan area can have several airports, and a link between two airports is not necessarily a link between two otherwise distinct city markets. A city-level analysis would need to group airports into metropolitan areas before counting connections. The airport-level map keeps that distinction visible at the places where each route begins and ends.
