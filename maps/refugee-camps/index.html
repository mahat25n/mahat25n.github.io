Refugee camps in sub-Saharan Africa

A refugee camp is often discussed as an emergency response. Its duration raises a different economic question: what happens when a temporary settlement becomes part of a region’s everyday economy? Housing, water, schools and access to work require decisions with much longer horizons than an initial relief operation.

Reading the map

The map follows camp sites in sub-Saharan Africa recorded in the African Refugee Camp Dataset between 1999 and 2024. Each point represents a site, with sub-camps and reopenings combined in the mapped preparation. Circle size records years in operation; the distinction between open and closed refers to the end of 2024. A large circle therefore represents duration, not a large refugee population.

The economic interpretation

For an economist, the relevant unit extends outside the camp boundary. Residents buy goods, seek employment and use infrastructure shared with neighbouring communities. These connections create questions about local prices, labour demand and public-service provision. The map identifies where such questions arise; it does not establish whether a camp raised incomes or strained services in a particular host community.

Long-lived sites make the distinction between humanitarian expenditure and development investment harder to sustain. A water network or school must be assessed against the people likely to use it over time, including host residents. The practical lesson is to investigate settlement histories before treating every camp as a short-lived interruption. Population histories, legal rights and local economic data would be needed to move from this geographic account to an assessment of welfare.
