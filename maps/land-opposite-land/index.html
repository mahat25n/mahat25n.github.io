Where land lies opposite land

Geographic distance is easy to describe and surprisingly easy to misinterpret. Two places can be physically far apart while remaining commercially well connected. Others can be much closer but separated by costly transport links. This map begins with a geometric extreme: the point directly opposite a location on Earth.

Reading the map

I used Natural Earth land polygons, transformed their coordinates to antipodal positions and compared the resulting geometry with land. Orange identifies land whose antipode also lies on land. The Equal Earth projection presents those overlaps in a single global view. This is a geometric exercise, not a dataset of travel routes or economic exchanges.

The economic interpretation

The economic connection lies in the distinction between physical and effective distance. Trade models often use distance as an indicator of the costs of moving goods, information and people. Actual costs depend on ports, transport services, border procedures and the nature of the product. Antipodal position says nothing about whether two places have a direct connection.

That limitation is the point of the exercise. A globe gives us a precise definition of remoteness in space, while economic remoteness depends on the network through which people transact. The map is useful as a visual prompt for that distinction. It does not imply that opposite regions trade little, share economic characteristics or face comparable development constraints.
