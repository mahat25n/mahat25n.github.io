Where the Tracks Divide

Two high-speed passenger services leave İstanbul along the same western corridor and then go in different directions. Near Polatlı, the route to Sivas continues via Ankara, Kırıkkale and Yozgat. The service to Karaman turns south toward Konya without calling at Ankara Gar. The upper panel shows that split. The lower panel shows the roads that connect many more towns than these two rail services can reach. A place can sit close to a line on the first map and depend on the second for most of its journeys. The economic question is how the two networks change the time, cost and reliability of reaching other people and markets. TCDD Taşımacılık high-speed services.

Why traffic along the shared corridor matters

Türkiye opened its first high-speed service between Ankara and Eskişehir in 2009. Later services extended the reach of that investment west to İstanbul and connected other cities. The Ministry of Transport and Infrastructure reports more than 12 million high-speed passenger journeys in 2025. About 6 million were on Ankara–İstanbul services and 2 million on İstanbul–Konya–Karaman services. The figures count journeys, including repeat travellers. The two services in the map illustrate how trains can share part of the infrastructure and then serve distinct markets. They form only part of the system counted by the ministry. Ministry of Transport and Infrastructure, 2025 rail figures.

High-speed rail has large fixed costs. The line, stations, power supply and signalling have to be paid for before the first passenger boards. Regular traffic across the same segment allows that cost to be spread across more journeys. Where several services share tracks, the benefits also depend on how well the timetable uses the available train paths. A well-timed mix of express and stopping services can serve major centres and intermediate cities. A congested junction can instead make delays spread from one service to another. The value of the connection near Polatlı therefore depends on the trains it can carry, their frequency and how reliably each service reaches its destination. International Transport Forum, The Economics of Investment in High-Speed Rail.

How rail changes a door-to-door journey

People choose between a train, coach, car and plane by assessing what the whole trip will cost them. Economists call the combination of money, time and inconvenience the generalised cost of travel. It includes the fare, the trip to the station, waiting and transfers at the far end. Reliability matters too. A traveller with a fixed appointment may build extra time into the journey when delays are hard to predict. A faster train reduces one part of that cost. A station that is hard to reach or a service with few useful departures can absorb much of the saving.

Consider a small firm in Eskişehir deciding whether to send someone to İstanbul to meet a customer. The firm weighs the cost of the ticket against staff time, the likelihood of a useful meeting and the prospect of returning the same day. An early departure and a suitable return might make the meeting possible without an overnight stay. A later or less predictable connection changes that calculation. The same logic applies to a student making a regular trip or a family visiting relatives. For some passengers, a lower fare does most to make the trip feasible. For others, the deciding factor is whether the departure and return fit around a working day.

These choices explain why passenger counts alone provide an incomplete account of the economic gains. A former coach traveller who switches to rail may save time. A new passenger may make a trip that previously cost too much. A third passenger may have moved from a conventional train and release a seat there. Each case has a different effect on passengers, operators and the rest of the network. Passenger surveys, service records and fare data can help establish where the journeys came from and which groups gained access.

Access to jobs, customers and services

A faster intercity service can widen the area in which households search for jobs and businesses look for customers. The effect is strongest for trips that become feasible at the times people actually need to travel. An occasional specialist appointment can justify a journey that would be too expensive for daily commuting. A company may arrange more frequent meetings with its suppliers before it changes where staff live. Employers benefit if they can recruit people with skills that were hard to find locally, while workers benefit if they can choose among more suitable jobs. Those gains depend on the type of journey, its frequency and the location of the station relative to the destination.

Better access works in both directions. A smaller city may attract weekend visitors or become easier to reach for a firm seeking lower premises costs. It may also lose some spending to a larger centre once shoppers can get there and back more easily. Land around a station may rise in value because access improved, with the gains accruing to owners and tenants potentially facing higher rents. Observing new building near a station would tell us where investment is appearing. To estimate what the railway added, we would also need to know which activity moved from elsewhere and what development would have occurred without the service.

Why the main roads still matter

The lower panel makes the difference in coverage visible. Motorways, trunk roads and primary roads form a much denser network across Türkiye than the two selected rail routes. They provide access to railway stations and carry trips for which there is no convenient rail service. A feeder bus connecting an outlying town to a station can extend the benefit of the railway to passengers who live well beyond the station neighbourhood. Its timetable matters. If a train arrives after the final connecting bus, the passenger may need a taxi or forego the trip. For a household without a car, the cost of that final connection can determine whether the new rail service is usable.

Freight raises a related problem at a different scale. The high-speed routes shown here carry passengers. A manufacturer moving a container needs a way to reach a freight terminal, get the load onto a train and collect it near its destination. On a long shipment, rail can carry the main leg, with trucks collecting and delivering the container. Each transfer adds handling and time. A short rail connection from a port or industrial area can be valuable if it removes an expensive transfer or makes shipment times more predictable. Türkiye’s Rail Logistics Improvement Project targets such connections at selected freight nodes. Its project documents describe intended improvements, which operators still have to turn into reliable services. World Bank, Rail Logistics Improvement Project.

The İstanbul Strait shows why capacity on one link matters well beyond the link itself. Freight trains using Marmaray have a limited night-time operating window, according to the appraisal of the İstanbul North Rail Crossing. In March 2026 the World Bank approved US$2 billion in financing for a proposed 127-kilometre electrified railway using the Yavuz Sultan Selim Bridge. The new crossing is intended to add passenger and freight capacity. For firms booking shipments across İstanbul, the relevant outcome will be whether they can get more dependable departure slots and shorter, more predictable journeys. The financing decision records a plan. Those outcomes can be assessed when the infrastructure and services are in use. Project environmental and social assessment · World Bank financing decision.

What makes a transport investment worth the cost

A good appraisal compares the new service with a credible alternative. On a busy corridor a new line might save enough passenger time, improve reliability and release enough capacity to cover the cost of construction and operation. Elsewhere, a signalling upgrade, more frequent departures or better station access might deliver much of the benefit for less. The comparison needs realistic assumptions about ridership and fares, maintenance over the life of the line, and the cost of land and construction. It must also account for differences in who gains. A high fare may improve operating revenue while limiting the number of workers and students who can use the time saving. International Transport Forum, The Economics of Investment in High-Speed Rail.

The emissions comparison also depends on what travellers would otherwise have done. An electric train can reduce emissions by drawing passengers from cars or flights, with the size of the saving depending on how full the train is and how electricity is generated. Building new tracks, bridges and tunnels uses materials and energy, so the comparison should include construction and maintenance as well as daily operations. Similarly, fewer cars on a corridor may bring changes in congestion and collision risk. How long those gains last depends partly on how other drivers respond to the road space that becomes available.

For Türkiye, an assessment of a new service would start with changes in fares, door-to-door travel times and the number of usable departures. Passenger records could then be matched with surveys of earlier travel choices and reasons for travel. A study of local employment or business activity would need comparable places and a view of how they were changing before the line opened. That comparison matters because growing cities may be chosen for service precisely because demand is already rising. At the scale of this map, the open question is how much of the apparent connection becomes affordable and reliable access for people and firms.

Reading the two panels

In the rail panel, cyan marks the common western corridor, violet the branch toward Sivas and amber the branch toward Karaman. I modelled 11 selected places and 10 schematic links in Gephi. The Polatlı marker represents the junction area. Several intermediate stops are omitted. In the road panel, gold shows motorways, turquoise trunk roads and violet primary roads. The 30,905 features in the OpenStreetMap extract are mapped segments, so a named road can contain many features. The night-light background helps orient the reader. Its intensity gives no measure of traffic, travel time or the economic return to a route. Road extract · NASA Black Marble.
