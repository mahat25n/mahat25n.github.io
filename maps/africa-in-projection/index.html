Africa in Projection

A lighthouse is built at one point on a coast, but its benefit reaches every vessel that can see it. A river ties a power station to farms downstream, and a city’s size tells us little about how many jobs its residents can reach. I made twelve views of Africa for the Projections theme of the 2026 30DayMapChallenge. Each uses a different map projection and follows a different feature into the economic decisions it helps explain.

How to read the atlas

The sheet compares all twelve views from directly above. In the animation, the view tilts to reveal the rendered surfaces. I prepared the geographic layers in QGIS and rendered the views in Blender. The height of a surface can represent elevation, a thematic value or a stylised base, depending on the layer. Each note below explains what that view measures.

Projection and subject change together across the atlas. When two panels differ, the subject, the projection and the rendering can all contribute. The projection matters most when reading shapes, comparing relative areas or judging apparent distance. The economic questions begin with the underlying features and the costs, benefits and connections they make possible. 30DayMapChallenge 2026 · PROJ projection reference.

Orthographic · Elevation

Look at the Ethiopian Highlands and then follow the descent toward the Horn of Africa. The difference in height is immediately visible. For a farmer trying to sell a perishable crop or a carrier deciding how many trips a truck can make in a day, what matters is how that change in height is crossed. A road climbing a steep grade can raise fuel use and slow heavy vehicles. A mountain pass can concentrate traffic onto a single route. The expense becomes especially visible when a storm cuts that route and the next usable crossing is several hours away.

From above, two towns may look equally close to a market. In one case there is a direct route with a reliable surface. In the other, vehicles descend into a valley, cross a river and climb the opposite side. The difference affects contracts as well as journeys. Buyers can offer lower farm-gate prices where spoilage and late delivery are more likely. A processor may locate on the more dependable side of a pass even if the other side grows more of the crop. Once warehouses and repair services gather around that choice, the original advantage can persist even after the road network improves.

That is why a road investment in difficult terrain is worth evaluating in all seasons. Resurfacing a long stretch may reduce ordinary travel time. Stabilising a short hillside or repairing a crossing may prevent a much larger loss on the days when the route would otherwise close. The distribution of benefits can differ as well. A project that makes freight cheaper on a major corridor may leave nearby villages isolated unless they have usable links into it. The World Bank’s Rural Access Index adds an important household perspective by measuring how many rural people live within two kilometres of an all-season road. Journey times and closure records would tell us what that access delivers. World Bank Rural Access Index.

There are gains from elevation, too. Cooler conditions can support crops with different growing requirements, and water descending from uplands can supply farms, cities and hydropower. Those advantages depend on local soils, rainfall and infrastructure. The same hillside that raises the cost of a road can make a crop viable or supply water to people far beyond the ridge. Evaluating a road purely on construction cost can miss the value of the places it connects. Valuing the highlands purely for their output can miss the cost of making their goods and services available elsewhere.

The Orthographic view presents a globe-like image, while the raised surface makes elevation differences more legible. The relief is exaggerated. Road grades require distances and heights taken along the routes people actually use. To investigate the connection between elevation and access, I would pair the terrain with road surfaces, market locations and travel times through wet and dry months. That is how a visible ridge becomes a question about who can reach a buyer, at what price, and throughout how much of the year. WorldClim elevation data.

Lambert Azimuthal Equal Area · Land cover

From this height, a colour change can represent a decision made by many people over many years. A patch of cropland may have expanded because a road made it cheaper to sell a harvest. A stretch of grassland may persist because livestock keepers can use it through several seasons. Where the map shows forest, value can come from timber, food, water regulation or uses that go unrecorded in ordinary market accounts. The same hectare can enter several economies at once.

Consider a proposal to bring more land into cultivation. The cultivator compares expected sales with the costs of clearing, inputs and labour. Households using the same land for grazing, fuel or forest products face a separate calculation. Farther downstream, people can bear the costs of changes in water quality and sediment. How much of that enters the decision depends on who has rights over the land, whose losses are recognised and who can negotiate. Measuring the value of a harvest helps answer one part of that question. Measuring what households and firms already receive from the landscape helps answer the rest. UN System of Environmental-Economic Accounting.

Physical land accounts offer a useful starting point. With comparable maps for two dates, they can show whether the area classified as cropland changed and which categories contributed to that change. A table of transitions is particularly useful. An increase in cropland can coexist with cultivation ending elsewhere. A single net figure hides both movements. Spatial detail also matters when the economic value of a change depends on where it occurs. Losing vegetation beside a reservoir and losing an equivalent area far from a water intake can produce quite different costs for a utility. UN land accounts.

Who captures the gain is a separate question from whether a land conversion pays. If a new road raises land values, an owner may be able to borrow against the increase. A tenant with a short lease may pay higher rent before seeing much improvement in farm income. A household with seasonal grazing rights may lose flexibility just when dry years make that flexibility valuable. In a local study I would put leases, customary uses and seasonal movements alongside the land-cover transitions. A change in the colour of a pixel says little about which of those interests has bargaining power.

The equal-area projection preserves relative areas, which makes it a useful choice for comparing the mapped extent of land-cover classes across Africa. The colours are selected categories from Global Land Cover 2000, a historical snapshot. The classification describes the surface and can combine several local uses within a single category. To answer a question about conversion today, I would use comparable observations across dates and check them against local evidence. The continental view helps choose the places to examine. The explanation comes from the sequence of investments, rights and decisions in those places. European Commission Joint Research Centre, Global Land Cover 2000.

Sinusoidal · Rivers and drainage basins

Follow one river upstream and the map starts to look like a network of people who may never meet. A reservoir operator watches inflows. Farmers decide whether to plant for the next season. A city buys chemicals to treat the water it draws downstream. The basin links these decisions through the movement of water. A change in one place can affect costs somewhere else even when the communities involved trade little with one another.

Timing is often where those costs arise. A dam can store water and release it when electricity prices are high or demand is greatest. Farmers downstream care about whether water arrives when crops need it. Two schedules can deliver the same volume over a year while producing different electricity revenues, harvests and flood exposures. Economically, a release schedule is part of the service a reservoir provides. Its value depends on whose needs it meets, when it meets them and what alternatives those users have.

There can be room for cooperation before anyone builds anything. Sharing rainfall, river-level and release data can give downstream users more time to prepare for floods or adjust irrigation plans. It also makes combined investments easier to assess. Suppose that changing the operation of an upstream reservoir costs the operator revenue but reduces expensive emergency measures downstream. A joint appraisal can put those gains and losses on the same table. Agreement still requires a credible way to make payments or share benefits, along with evidence that the operating rules are followed. The Nile Basin Initiative’s regional investment programme illustrates how countries can prepare projects around such cross-border benefits. Nile River Basin Investment Programme.

Basin thinking also changes how we evaluate water quality. A town might save money by doing little about wastewater today, while communities downstream pay more for treatment or lose income from fishing and recreation. Cost accounts for each town can look reasonable on their own. At basin scale the costs add up differently. The same principle applies to investments that trap sediment or restore a floodplain. Some of the value may accrue to places where the project cannot collect fees. That is a reason to trace benefits before deciding how the work should be financed.

I paired Natural Earth river lines with level-two HydroBASINS boundaries. The lines identify major mapped waterways and the polygons show a broad division into drainage areas. The Sinusoidal projection preserves relative area, useful when comparing the geographical scale of catchments. Water quantity, seasonal flow and withdrawals would need separate measurements. A line through a dry region can be economically important even if few other rivers appear nearby. To compare two proposals, I would trace their effects through the basin and model who receives water of what quality, with what reliability, and at which times of year. That connects the branching pattern on the map to decisions households, farms and utilities actually face. Natural Earth physical vectors · HydroBASINS technical documentation.

Mollweide · Annual rainfall

Rain is valuable when it reaches a crop, a pasture or a water store at the right time. What a household can do with it also depends on whether it has a buyer, money for seed and somewhere to keep a harvest. The green relief traces long-run rainfall totals across Africa. It invites a practical question. How does a wetter climate change the range of farm investments households can afford to make?

Where crops are grown with rainfall, the long-run water budget helps shape the choice of crops, seed and planting area. A household that expects enough moisture may be willing to spend more on seed and fertiliser because those inputs have a better chance of paying off. Yet it still faces prices at harvest and the risk that rain comes too late. One response is to spread land across crops with different requirements. Another is to invest in water storage. A farm with limited cash may keep some land in a lower-return crop because it can better withstand a poor season. The economic cost of uncertain water includes opportunities forgone before any loss occurs.

Irrigation changes the calculation. A farmer who can buy or pump water at the right moment may be able to grow a more valuable crop or deliver when prices are higher. The investment also brings electricity or fuel bills, equipment maintenance and sometimes collective decisions about canals or water allocations. A project that works for one farm can become harder to sustain if many users pump from the same source. To value irrigation, I would compare the incremental income with costs over the life of the system, including the cost of water to other users and the risk of unreliable supply.

Harvest size can also change the price a farm receives. If many nearby growers harvest together and the crop is expensive to move or store, prices can fall even in a productive year. The potential gains from more reliable rainfall then depend in part on the roads, buyers and storage facilities available to the region. A rainfall improvement and a marketing improvement could reinforce each other. This is why I would compare farm-gate prices and quantities together. A change in kilograms harvested captures a different part of the economic outcome from a change in what families can earn.

On the map, higher thematic relief represents a larger annual total, obtained by summing average monthly WorldClim precipitation for 1970–2000. The Mollweide projection preserves relative area, useful for comparing the extent of broad rainfall zones. These historical means provide a common reference. For a study of current farm decisions, I would add recent local weather, soils, crop calendars and input and output prices. The wettest-three-months view asks how annual rainfall is distributed within the year. Together the views suggest different ways a place can be short of usable water, each with different implications for spending on irrigation, storage or other farm inputs. WorldClim climate normals and units.

Robinson · City populations

When a city appears as a tall tower, I find it useful to ask what its residents can reach before the working day begins. A large population creates the possibility of a large labour market. For someone spending two hours on a bus each way, the accessible labour market can still be quite small. The two scales matter together. The city’s population describes the number of potential workers and customers, while the transport and housing systems determine how much of that potential people can use.

Proximity can pay in several ways. A business can specialise when it has enough customers to support a narrow service. A firm can find workers with particular skills and share suppliers with other firms. A worker can look for a better match without having to move to another region. The benefits depend on how people and businesses are arranged. If the travel cost between a residential district and an employment centre rises, the number of workers in the city remains the same on the map even as the pool available to a firm shrinks. In its study of African cities, the World Bank distinguishes the concentration of people from the connections that make urban economies work. World Bank, African Cities: Opening Doors to the World.

Housing affects who benefits when those connections improve. A new route can make more jobs reachable from a neighbourhood. Landlords may then charge more for housing near its stops. A homeowner can gain from a rise in property value, while a renter’s shorter journey may be partly offset by higher rent. Some tenants may move farther out. Tracking rents and residential moves alongside commuting times would show how the benefit is divided. A citywide average can easily conceal that distinction, particularly where many residents have informal tenure or jobs that require trips outside standard peak hours.

The same idea extends beyond city boundaries. A large city buys food and other inputs from its hinterland. Small producers gain a potentially larger market, but they need regular and affordable transport, working capital and buyers willing to deal with smaller volumes. It matters where intermediate markets and warehouses sit. A connection between a rural district and one of the city’s supply hubs may bring producers into that market faster than a route to the administrative centre. City population alone gives little guidance about which link would do more.

In this frame I used Natural Earth’s selected populated places. Taller towers represent higher population estimates for represented places. The Robinson projection gives a continental overview, and the spacing of the towers changes with the projection. The height of a tower has no direct economic interpretation beyond the population encoded. To measure urban market access, I would use jobs, firms or customers reachable within specific costs or travel times, with a clear definition of the urban area. The towers help locate the question. Travel, housing and employment data determine who can take advantage of the city’s scale. Natural Earth populated places.

Plate Carrée · Coastal lighthouses

In this view, Africa’s land recedes into blue and a chain of small lights traces parts of the coast. Each glow represents a selected mapped lighthouse. The light makes an interesting economic object. After a light is operating, one vessel seeing it generally leaves it just as visible to another. Collecting payment from each vessel at the moment it sees the signal would be difficult. The benefits can be widespread even when the costs of maintenance and operation are concentrated at a single site.

This is the problem behind a long debate about lighthouses and public goods. Ronald Coase’s study of historical England looked at how provision was organised in practice. In some cases private operators received authority to collect lighthouse dues at ports. Ships paid through an existing point of contact, giving the operator a way to recover costs. The historical example is interesting because it shows that the technical properties of the signal and the institutions used to provide it are separate questions. The marks on this map do not report how the lights were paid for. They do make the financing question visible along an extended coastline. Ronald Coase, “The Lighthouse in Economics”.

Who ultimately bears the cost is another question. Where fees are collected from vessels or shippers, some of the cost can reach importers and consumers through freight prices. The share passed along depends on competition and the options available to firms and households. A light may also serve users who rarely pay port dues, including small fishing vessels. Public funding can recognise benefits shared across many users, while a vessel charge can tie some revenue to commercial use. Comparing the two arrangements requires information about who uses the service and how each payment would affect their choices.

Maintenance belongs in that comparison. A signal that is reliable in poor weather has a different value from one that frequently fails at the moments it is needed. The relevant benefit may include avoided disruptions to port activity and lower risk on an approach used by many vessels. It may also depend on charts, buoys and electronic aids already in service. To compare maintenance at two sites, I would look for vessel movements, outage records and accident exposure. The density of points along a coast is a poor substitute for any of those measures.

Plate Carrée is the Equidistant Cylindrical projection with the equator as its standard parallel. Its simple grid lets the distribution of coastal points carry the scene. The layer contains 323 selected named OpenStreetMap features from the Lighthouses Worldwide extract. Gaps reflect the selection and availability of mapped records. An economic history of maritime safety could begin by matching those records to port archives and official inventories. A study of current services would also need operating status and vessel routes. Both would address a question the illuminated coast suggests. How do users pay for a service whose value extends far beyond the point where it is supplied? Mercator · Lakes and reservoirs

For a household beside a lake, the shoreline can be a workplace, a source of food and a route to market. For an electricity utility, a reservoir may be a way to store water for periods of high demand. A blue shape on a continental map can therefore sit at the centre of very different economies. The shared resource is water, but the service people value may be fish, electricity, transport or reliable supply through a dry season.

Take a fish landing site. Much of its local value is created after the catch comes ashore. People sort, process, preserve and transport the fish. An improvement in cold storage can reduce losses and let sellers wait for a buyer who pays a better price. It can also increase pressure on fish stocks if access to the fishery is hard to manage. Valuing the investment means following both changes. A larger traded catch can raise incomes in the short run while weakening the resource on which those incomes depend. The FAO describes why these questions matter for food, jobs and livelihoods in inland fisheries. FAO inland fisheries, food and livelihoods.

A reservoir makes the trade-offs especially concrete. Operators can store water for hydropower, irrigation or municipal supply. Drawing it down for electricity today may leave less flexibility later in the season. Holding more water can support one use while reducing capacity to absorb a flood. Reservoir operations can also change access to fisheries, farmland or settlements around and downstream from the water. An appraisal that counts electricity sales and construction costs leaves out part of the decision. The scope should extend to the people who gain or lose from altered flows and to the alternatives available to them.

Water quality introduces another set of costs. Where wastewater, nutrients or sediment enter a lake, the households or firms responsible may be well removed from the communities paying for treatment or losing fishing income. A municipality may be able to finance action within its own border while having little control over inflows from elsewhere. Measuring loads at their source and following them to the users affected gives a basis for sharing costs. It also helps distinguish between changes a local authority can deliver alone and those that require a wider agreement.

The map draws generalised Natural Earth outlines of lakes and reservoirs. The Mercator view is spherical and conformal. It preserves local angles and changes the scale of area with latitude. Comparing the size of water bodies would require measuring source geometry in suitable area units. Storage and water levels would require further data. Two reservoirs with similar surface areas can have different capacity and operating rules, and a lake with many landing sites can matter to households far from the shore. I would link the mapped outlines to the users and infrastructure around them before assigning an economic value. Natural Earth lakes and reservoirs · PROJ, Mercator.

Cylindrical Equal Area · Wettest three months

Two farms can receive the same amount of rain in an average year and face quite different decisions. If one receives most of it over three months, the window for planting and establishing crops can be tight. The other may have more room to plant at different times and spread out the demand for labour. The pink layer puts that difference in view. It shows the share of each location’s annual rainfall that falls in its wettest run of three consecutive months.

Concentration affects the timing of cash as well as the timing of work. Households may need seed, fertiliser and hired labour around the same few weeks, when income from the last harvest is running low. The value of short-term credit depends partly on whether it arrives before that window closes. If the planting window is missed, an otherwise affordable loan loses much of its value. At harvest, this schedule can run in reverse. Many producers may sell together to meet expenses, even if prices tend to improve later. A store that keeps grain dry, a loan secured against stored grain or a dependable buyer can change how much of the harvest is sold immediately.

Rain concentrated in a short season also raises a question about insurance design. An index policy can pay when observed rainfall falls below a trigger measured at a weather station or from a grid. Trigger timing matters. A policy based on the whole season can pay poorly when a short dry spell during germination causes a loss and rain later makes the seasonal total look adequate. A policy can also miss losses because the rainfall estimate differs from what happened on a particular farm. That gap between the loss and the payout is one form of basis risk. Comparing crop calendars, farm outcomes and local weather is part of deciding whether an index is useful to the people paying for it. World Bank technical report on agricultural finance and insurance in Uganda.

I would read this map with the annual-rainfall view open. A high share can describe a place with substantial rain concentrated in a short period. It can also describe a dry place where a little rain accounts for much of the yearly total. These settings present different choices about drainage, water storage and the risk of running short. The ability to hold water through a dry period depends on soils, losses from storage, rights to withdraw and the amount users can afford to invest.

The share is calculated from WorldClim’s monthly climate normals for 1970–2000, using the wettest consecutive three-month period at each location. Areas under 50 millimetres per year are masked. The Behrmann form of Cylindrical Equal Area uses standard parallels at 30° north and south, preserving relative area. The map measures typical concentration within a year. To assess lending or insurance, I would add year-by-year weather and crop losses, then test how well a proposed contract would have served households in seasons that differed from the average. WorldClim monthly climate normals · PROJ, Cylindrical Equal Area.

Winkel Tripel · Solar radiation

In the gold frame, sunlight is a resource spread across the landscape. An electricity project has to turn that broad resource into power at a particular site and deliver it to someone who will pay for it. Those steps narrow the set of attractive locations considerably. A bright area with a distant grid connection can face a larger bill for transmission than a slightly less sunny site close to an industrial buyer. Both the sale price and the cost of reaching that buyer matter.

Panels cost money up front and produce electricity over many years. An estimate of their output starts from available radiation and then depends on orientation, module temperature, equipment losses and site conditions. Dust and cleaning costs can affect the calculation in dry areas. The decision also depends on how much electricity a buyer will accept. A plant with strong midday production may have to cut output if the local network is congested. Comparing two sites on sunlight alone leaves out the value of an available connection and the hours when production can actually be sold. Global Solar Atlas methodology.

The shape of demand changes the economics. A mill that runs during daylight may be able to use much of a nearby plant’s output directly. A village where demand peaks in the evening needs another source after sunset or a way to store part of the day’s production. The value of storage follows from those hours. For a mini-grid serving households and shops, productive daytime uses can help spread fixed costs over more sales. For a large utility project, transmission access and the terms of the power purchase agreement can have as much influence on financing as differences in radiation across candidate sites.

Who carries the risk can alter the price at which a project becomes feasible. A lender considers whether the buyer can pay over the term of the contract and how delays in interconnection would be handled. Higher financing costs can make a project with an excellent resource expensive per unit of electricity delivered. A comparison of investment options would therefore estimate output at each location and examine the surrounding market. That includes the price buyers face today, whether another supplier can serve them and how much it would cost to deliver power when they need it.

The mapped layer averages WorldClim monthly solar-radiation normals for 1970–2000 and presents them in megajoules per square metre per day. Winkel Tripel is a compromise projection used here for a continental view. The gold surface helps identify broad areas with stronger radiation. For project appraisal I would add recent, site-specific solar data, grid capacity, demand by hour and contract terms. The map starts the search with a physical resource. The economic comparison turns on the expected cost and value of electricity that can be delivered. WorldClim radiation data · PROJ, Winkel Tripel.

Aitoff · Distance to coastline

The violet surface rises inland. It represents the shortest distance to a mapped coast. From a trader’s point of view, the important distance is paid in freight charges, time, fees and the cost of goods sitting in transit. Those costs can produce a very different map. A warehouse close to the sea can still face a long trip to a working port. An inland city can have a relatively dependable link to one if the road or railway works well and the documents and border crossings are predictable.

For a firm ordering inputs, uncertainty can cost as much as a long average trip. If suppliers sometimes deliver late, the firm may hold extra stock to keep production running. That ties up money it could use to expand or pay workers. An importer with limited credit may order in smaller batches, turn down time-sensitive business or choose a more expensive route because it is reliable. The resulting cost may never appear on a freight invoice. It can show up in inventory accounts, production interruptions and the kinds of orders firms are able to accept.

Border procedures are part of the same calculation. A shipment travelling overland may pass through several agencies on the way to a port. Improving one stretch of road can save time, yet the cargo can still lose days waiting at a crossing or terminal. Where the delay depends on schedules and paperwork, an agreement between agencies could have a greater effect on delivery times than faster travel between them. UNCTAD’s work on landlocked developing countries treats route reliability and transit procedures as components of logistics costs. UNCTAD, The Way to the Ocean: Transit Corridors Servicing the Trade of Landlocked Developing Countries.

Product characteristics help determine who benefits from a shorter or more reliable journey. For a bulky, low-value export, a reduction in freight charges can change whether the producer can compete at all. A seller of fresh produce can be more sensitive to delay and spoilage. A manufacturer waiting for a particular component may value a predictable delivery window most of all. To compare corridor projects, I would look at which goods move, which firms can use the service and how total costs change across different shipment sizes and seasons. A single average rate can hide large differences in the gains.

The distances in the Aitoff view are approximate great-circle distances to the nearest Natural Earth coastline. I used the coastline as a physical reference, so the nearest point can be far from a port that handles freight. The projection displays the resulting surface after the distances have been calculated. To compare access I would trace routes to ports using observed charges, border times and the frequency of services. Comparing those results with the violet surface would show where geographic separation and effective access tell different stories. Natural Earth coastline.

Stereographic · Terrain slope

In the grayscale view, the feature is the rate at which elevation changes across the ground. A high plateau can contain gently sloping farmland, and a low hill can have steep sides. That distinction matters to a farmer choosing where to cultivate. Steeper land can be harder to work with machinery. It can also require investments to keep soil and water on the plot. How much those investments are worth depends on what the land produces, how long the farmer expects to use it and what happens after rain leaves the field.

Terraces, ground cover and planting along contours can slow runoff under suitable conditions. They can also use labour or land that would otherwise produce a crop. A household may face a choice between meeting that cost now and receiving a steadier yield over several seasons. Access to credit and security of tenure shape the choice. A tenant with a short lease may have little incentive to pay for work that largely benefits a future occupier. A farmer with secure long-term rights may still postpone it if the main cost comes during the busiest part of the growing season. FAO, soil conservation and erosion control.

The farmer’s private return can also differ from the return to people downstream. Soil washed off several plots may collect in a canal or reservoir. The operator pays to remove sediment or loses capacity, even though the work that could reduce runoff lies elsewhere. There is a case for sharing conservation costs where the expected downstream savings justify it. Doing that well requires identifying where sediment originates. Paying every hillside farm the same amount could spend money on slopes whose runoff rarely reaches the infrastructure the programme is meant to protect.

A comparison would start with the condition of the soil, what is planted on it and the way runoff moves during storms. I would then consider where the sediment ends up and whether crops, roads, canals or reservoirs are affected. Some steep ground is well protected by vegetation. Ground with a more moderate slope may lose soil when it is bare during heavy rain. Measuring changes over time would help separate the effect of conservation work from differences in rainfall or land management. The economic calculation would include maintenance and ask who saves money, whose yields improve and how long those gains take to arrive.

This Stereographic view uses slope derived from WorldClim elevation on a 0.2-degree grid. It captures broad changes in terrain that can be seen across a continent. Gradients within a field can vary considerably inside one cell. Stereographic is conformal, and the displayed gradient was calculated from elevation data before rendering. The map can guide a regional search for places to examine. Before choosing a conservation investment, I would use finer terrain data and field observations to connect the gradient to soil loss and to the costs people bear. WorldClim elevation data · PROJ, Stereographic.

Equal Earth · Mean wind speed

The final map brings the eye to places where the long-run mean wind speed is higher. I read it as the beginning of an investment question. A turbine earns revenue from electricity delivered over many years, so the value of a windy site depends on the speeds it actually experiences, when those speeds occur and how much it costs to connect to customers. A bright area can look promising at continental scale while leaving most of those questions open.

Two sites with the same mean speed can produce different amounts of electricity. A turbine generates little or no power below certain speeds, produces more as wind rises through part of its operating range and stops if conditions exceed its safety limit. The distribution of wind over time therefore matters. So do measurements at turbine hub height and the effect of nearby terrain. A project assessment would use a suitable turbine power curve and site data to estimate output, allowing for losses and periods when equipment is unavailable. The average on this map is useful for asking where to look more closely. Global Wind Atlas methodology.

There is another difference between electricity generated and electricity valued. A site that often produces at hours of high demand may help meet demand when other sources are costly. Wind farms in similar weather patterns can produce heavily at the same time, reducing prices or straining transmission capacity. A region with a lower average speed can still contribute to the system if its wind tends to arrive at different times. To evaluate that contribution, I would compare hourly production across sites with demand, the capacity of transmission lines and the output of other generators.

The contracts stand between those physical patterns and the decision to build. Financing depends on how much revenue can be expected and who absorbs production shortfalls or curtailment. Local economic returns depend on land arrangements, jobs and the use of roads or other infrastructure. A developer can see an attractive revenue stream while land users face disrupted access or a change in rents. An appraisal can compare those costs and benefits explicitly. It would distinguish returns during construction from income and obligations that continue after the turbines begin operating.

The Equal Earth projection preserves relative area, which helps compare the extent of broad resource zones. The surface shows mean wind speed in metres per second from monthly WorldClim climate normals for 1970–2000. A bankable estimate would need recent observations, wind at hub height and a clear view of the electricity market the project would serve. This brings the atlas back to a question posed by several of the other layers. Geography shows where a resource is available. Networks, costs and institutions determine who can use it and what they can earn from it. WorldClim wind climatology · PROJ, Equal Earth.
