Europe’s Electric Arteries

Europe’s transmission grid connects places where electricity is generated to places where it is used. Those places do not necessarily coincide. A coastal wind farm, an inland industrial centre and a city with a high evening demand can all depend on the same network. The map makes that shared infrastructure visible. It follows transmission lines across borders and over a terrain surface that shows the mountains, plains and coastlines through which the network runs.

The data behind the map

The lines and substation nodes come from the OpenStreetMap-based electricity network prepared for PyPSA-Eur, release 0.8. I retained the geometries and attributes of the original network. It contains 9,374 alternating-current lines, 39 high-voltage direct-current links and 7,017 plotted substation nodes. The AC lines are grouped into three nominal-voltage bands. Purple identifies the DC links, which are shown as a separate technology. The plotted substation nodes belong to the processed network model. Nearby facilities can be combined into one node. PyPSA-Eur network data ↗

The terrain comes from NOAA’s ETOPO 2022 global relief model. I prepared the network overlay in QGIS and rendered the terrain in Blender. The orthographic terrain render and the vector overlay share the same extent and projection. Elevation therefore adds geographic context without shifting a line or substation horizontally. The relief is exaggerated to make mountain ranges easier to see at continental scale. In a close view, the resulting surface is smoother than the terrain itself. NOAA’s ETOPO 2022 documentation ↗

What an interconnected grid makes possible

Transmission gives electricity systems a way to share resources across regions. When demand rises in one area, generation in another area can help meet it if the lines have spare capacity. The same connection can carry power in the opposite direction under different operating conditions. With wind and solar generation, the ability to exchange electricity can be especially useful because the timing and location of output vary with the weather. The map shows the infrastructure that makes such exchanges possible.

Where lines cross a national border, the operational question is how much power can be transferred at a particular time. That depends on the condition of the network, the equipment, the available capacity and the location of generation and demand. A voltage band describes the nominal operating voltage. It cannot be read as a transfer limit or a measure of power flowing through a line. Nor does a cluster of bright lines establish that one region has more reliable supply than another.

Reading gaps and corridors

The strongest visual contrast is between the dense lines across much of continental Europe and the sparser connections toward the margins of the map. Some of that contrast reflects the dataset’s coverage. The source excludes several countries and the underlying OpenStreetMap record can be incomplete. From a planning perspective, the mapped network is a starting point for asking where corridors converge and where systems connect. Answering where congestion occurs would require operating and market data for specific hours, along with a model of the grid under those conditions.

The terrain also changes the way the grid is read. A line on either side of the Alps may appear close to another on the page, even where the routes follow different valleys or connect to different substations. At this scale, visible crossings cannot establish that two circuits share a junction. The model’s substation and line records are needed to determine which connections are represented.
