Where submarine cables come ashore

Digital services depend on physical infrastructure. International connections come ashore at identifiable places, bringing the geography of the coast into activities that appear independent of distance. The economic interest is not only where cables arrive, but how concentrated those arrival points are.

Reading the map

I used TeleGeography’s submarine-cable landing-point data and DBSCAN spatial clustering to group nearby locations. Colours represent the number of landing points in a cluster; grey marks points outside those groups. The Robinson projection provides the global view. The underlying inventory includes existing and planned infrastructure, and the locations are approximate.

The economic interpretation

A cluster counts landing points, not bandwidth, internet users or the value of digital trade. Several nearby points may reflect a concentration of infrastructure without providing independent routes all the way to inland users. A proposed landing point also does not deliver the same current service as an operating connection. These distinctions matter before interpreting a large symbol as an economic advantage.

Concentration poses a familiar economic trade-off. Shared infrastructure and proximity can reduce connection costs, while dependence on a limited set of locations can create common exposure. Whether a coastal connection benefits inland firms depends on domestic transmission networks, competition, electricity and affordability. The map locates the coastal part of that question. It cannot, by itself, establish the quality or resilience of the service people receive.
