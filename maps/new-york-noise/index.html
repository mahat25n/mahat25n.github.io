Day 11 · Senses: Sound · 2026 collection

New York City: nighttime noise reports

Noise is an economic problem because its costs often fall on someone other than the person or activity producing it. A resident may bear the disturbance while another person receives the benefit of transport, construction or entertainment. The difficult policy question is how to manage those competing uses of urban space.

The data behind the map

I mapped NYC 311 noise service requests submitted during July 2025 between 11 pm and 7 am, using the source’s local timestamps. Located reports were grouped into hexagons. Purple indicates fewer reports, with pink, orange and yellow marking higher counts. Python handled the filtering, spatial aggregation and map rendering.

Complaints and the costs of noise

The resulting pattern describes reporting, not measured sound. Several requests may concern one event, and the time of a complaint is not necessarily the time the noise began. Differences in population, willingness to report and access to the reporting system can also affect the counts. A cell with no mapped reports should not be interpreted as silent.

Concentrated reports suggest places for closer investigation, not a ranking of neighbourhood welfare. Comparing reports with sound measurements, land use, population and enforcement records would help separate exposure from reporting behaviour. That distinction matters if the map is used to allocate inspection resources or evaluate whether a policy actually reduced disturbance.
