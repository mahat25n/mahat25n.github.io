Day 09 · Urban / Rural · 2026 collection

Where people live in Türkiye

The boundary of a province is not the boundary of a city’s economy. Households, jobs and transport connections form patterns within and across administrative areas. Looking at population in small geographic cells provides a different view from comparing provincial totals.

The data behind the map

For the national panel, I used GHS-WUP-POP R2025A at 1 km resolution; the İstanbul panel uses GHS-POP R2023A at 100 m resolution. Both represent the 2025 population epoch. Python converts population counts to density and renders a smoothed, shaded surface. Its relief represents population, not terrain. Because the panels use different resolutions and releases, their small-scale textures are not directly comparable.

Population and access to services

Population concentration creates questions about access and capacity. A dense area may support frequent public transport and nearby services, but the population map alone cannot tell us whether either is available. A sparsely populated area may face a different problem: providing essential services across greater distances. Neither pattern establishes whether residents are economically better off.

For public investment, the useful comparison is between where people live and where opportunities and services are located. Schools, hospitals, employment and commuting data would add the missing parts. The İstanbul inset helps expose differences that disappear at national scale. It encourages a finer reading of urban and rural space without confusing population density with productivity, prosperity or service quality.
