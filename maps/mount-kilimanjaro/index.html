Mount Kilimanjaro, Africa’s Highest Peak

Mount Kilimanjaro rises above the plains of northeastern Tanzania, close to the Kenyan border. Its highest point, Uhuru Peak on Kibo, reaches 5,895 metres, the highest elevation in Africa. Kibo, Mawenzi and Shira are the three volcanic centres visible in the relief. The map starts with this close view, then places the mountain within Tanzania and Tanzania within Africa. From Moshi, near the southern foot, the change in elevation that fits inside the top panel can take days to cross on foot. That distance between the plain and the summit is part of what visitors travel to experience. UNESCO World Heritage Centre.

Why the climb draws international visitors

A visitor on the southern slopes can pass from farms and settlements into mountain forest, then through heath and moorland toward the alpine desert below the summit. The shape of the mountain makes each of those stages visible at the scale of a single expedition. Routes such as Marangu and Machame offer different approaches, while the decision to allow time for acclimatisation changes the number of nights, the equipment carried and the crew needed. Tanzania National Parks also records the significance of the mountain to Chagga communities, including traditional practices associated with Marangu. The summit attracts people from abroad, and the lower slopes give them reasons to learn about the part of Tanzania in which the climb takes place. Tanzania National Parks.

Being Africa’s highest mountain gives Kilimanjaro a place in the global market for mountain travel. A climber planning a trip around one summit can also book nights in Moshi, visit a farm on the lower slopes or take a second trip to the northern safari circuit. The economic gain from those choices depends on how visitors use their time. A traveller who adds a day in the region creates an extra sale for an accommodation provider and may purchase a meal or local tour. A visitor on a tight itinerary can spend the same amount of time on the mountain and have very little contact with nearby businesses outside the operator’s package.

Where the money from a climb goes

A climbing package brings several markets into one purchase. A guide is paid for route knowledge and decisions about safety. Porters and cooks carry supplies and provide meals over several days. Hotels sell rooms before and after the climb, drivers connect them to the park gates, and equipment providers rent or repair gear. Those businesses then decide where to buy food, fuel and other supplies. The decision to source fresh produce nearby, for instance, can turn part of a tour operator’s order into recurring sales for a wholesaler or grower. The World Bank’s review of tourism in Tanzania identifies stronger connections between tourism firms and local suppliers as a way to retain more of the value generated by travel. Tanzania Economic Update.

The package price tells us how much the visitor paid. To understand incomes around the mountain, we also need to know where the package was sold, who owns the operator and lodge, what the crew earned and where suppliers are based. Two trips at the same price can therefore leave different amounts of income in Tanzania, and two trips with similar Tanzanian spending can distribute it differently between workers, business owners and the park. For a porter, the number of paid trips in a season and the expenses incurred on each trip matter more than the price on a foreign booking site. Pay, meals and equipment are part of the cost of delivering the climb, and the quality of that work is part of the value the visitor buys. Local efforts to monitor porter pay, loads and working conditions show how a buyer can learn more about the service behind the package price. Kilimanjaro Responsible Trekking Organization.

What the national figures tell us

Tanzania recorded about 2.14 million international visitor arrivals and US$3.90 billion in tourism earnings in 2024. The national exit survey reports those figures for Tanzania as a whole. They include many destinations and travel purposes. Spending by overseas visitors brings foreign exchange into the country, while domestic tourism also supports jobs and sales by moving spending within Tanzania. To estimate Kilimanjaro’s own contribution, an analyst would need park visits and the allocation of spending across the trip, including cases where the same visitor climbs the mountain and visits other parts of the country. 2024 International Visitors’ Exit Survey.

Tourism income can also be seasonal and exposed to shocks abroad. Long-haul travel becomes harder for households to buy when airfares rise or incomes fall in their home countries. A tour operator can see bookings drop months before a planned departure, and seasonal workers may feel the change in fewer paid days. Tanzania’s experience during the pandemic illustrates how a loss of international demand reached businesses that supplied tourism as well as those selling trips. It also explains the value of a mix of customers and activities for businesses at the foot of the mountain. Tanzania Economic Update.

Conservation and the economics of access

The wild setting is part of what makes the climb valuable. The park incurs real costs to keep trails passable, manage waste and organise rescue in steep terrain. A route can become congested at camps or on the final ascent even when other parts of the park have room. That makes the timing and distribution of visits important to management. A rise in park receipts can coexist with heavier maintenance costs at a few sites. Recording visitor numbers by route and season alongside those costs would help show where an additional booking can be accommodated and where the pressure is already high.

The mountain forest also has value to people who never buy a park ticket. It protects habitat and helps regulate water moving from the slopes to the settlements and farms below. A count of climbers or the revenue from a gate therefore captures only one use of the mountain. UNESCO’s listing recognises the volcanic massif and its ecosystems as a site of international significance. Locally, the test is whether the economic returns from access help pay for the conditions that make the mountain worth visiting and support the workers and communities that receive the visitors. UNESCO World Heritage Centre.

Reading the relief
