Day 23 · Senses: Taste · 2026 collection

A Taste of Earth

A meal brings different kinds of work to the same table. Some dishes begin with crops that households grow. Others depend on fishing communities, food processors or restaurants. I chose five foods from UNESCO’s Intangible Cultural Heritage records to compare how ingredients reach cooks, how people earn from preparing food, and how communities pass on the skills that give a dish its taste.

How to read the animation

The opening Mollweide equal-area map shows all five stops. The camera then flies between them on an orthographic globe before returning to the world map. Each pin marks a selected place associated with a tradition that extends across a wider area. The blue lights represent nighttime illumination from NASA’s 2016 Black Marble imagery.

Mexico · Maize tortillas from field to table

UNESCO’s Mexican inscription covers a food system, including cultivation, cooking and ritual, with Michoacán as its example. Maize, beans and chillies form the basis of that system. The milpa brings maize and other crops into the same farming system. Once the maize reaches the kitchen, cooks treat it in an alkaline solution before grinding it into dough. That process, known as nixtamalization, changes the properties of the grain and helps turn it into a dough suitable for tortillas. A tortilla therefore depends on skills that begin in the field and continue through preparation. UNESCO, 2010

Tortillas are a staple and also form part of Day of the Dead offerings. Daily meals give people frequent opportunities to practise making them. Offerings give the same food a place in family and public life that goes beyond everyday consumption. UNESCO describes collectives of women cooks and other practitioners in Michoacán and across Mexico. A tortilla sold in a market records a transaction, while the knowledge needed to produce it also comes from work and teaching within families and communities.

Peru · Ceviche links the fishing boat to the kitchen

Ceviche brings together artisanal fishers, farming families and cooks. Fish provides one set of ingredients, and farms provide produce that accompanies the dish. Families prepare it at home and cooks sell it in cevicherías. The resulting recipes vary across Peru because communities use different ingredients and techniques. Ceviche is also eaten at the Feast of Saint Peter, the patron saint of artisanal fishers. The celebration connects the people who make a living from fishing with an occasion on which families gather to eat. UNESCO, 2023

For a cook, access to the catch shapes which versions of ceviche can be prepared. For a fisher, restaurants and households provide buyers. A decline in a fish stock can therefore affect earnings at sea and the ingredients available in kitchens. UNESCO documents community measures to protect fish species used in ceviche. The economic point is that the continuity of the dish depends in part on how communities manage a shared resource. Lima is a reference point on the map for practices documented across the country.

Côte d’Ivoire · Attiéké turns cassava into a source of income

Among Côte d’Ivoire’s lagoon communities, making attiéké involves grinding cassava, mixing it with fermented cassava and steaming the resulting grains. Producers cool and pack it for distribution. UNESCO describes a division of work in which men take part in growing, harvesting, transporting and grinding cassava, while women traditionally prepare, preserve and sell attiéké. The finished food is an everyday staple, a restaurant meal and part of weddings, baptisms and funerals. UNESCO, 2024

Processing gives producers a way to earn from the work that turns the root into a ready-to-eat food. Younger producers learn by watching and working with experienced family and community members. UNESCO documents cooperatives and workshops that support training and production. Its decision also asks Côte d’Ivoire to monitor the risks of over-commercialization. As demand grows, the practical question is who captures the returns from expanded production and whether the women who hold and teach the skills keep a say in how attiéké is made and sold.

Ukraine · Borscht depends on people being able to cook together

Families make borscht for everyday meals and serve it to guests and at weddings. Children learn by helping prepare it, and different households pass on their own versions. UNESCO placed the culture of Ukrainian borscht cooking on the List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in Need of Urgent Safeguarding in 2022. Its decision describes how war and displacement limited access to local vegetables and disrupted occasions on which people used to cook and eat together. UNESCO, 2022

Displacement changes what households can grow or buy, who they live near and how often relatives can gather. A family can still make borscht in a new place, adapting it to the ingredients at hand. A child may have fewer chances to learn a particular family version through repeated meals with grandparents. A written recipe can preserve a set of instructions. The transmission of a cooking practice also requires the time, ingredients and contact that let people learn by doing.

Thailand · Tomyum Kung travels through kitchens and schools

Tomyum Kung developed among Buddhist riverside communities in Thailand’s Central Plains, where freshwater prawns were available. Lemongrass, galangal and other herbs help give the soup its flavour. Families teach people to select and combine ingredients. UNESCO also records how restaurants and educational institutions carry that knowledge to other cooks. The soup is now served in Thailand and abroad. UNESCO, 2024

A restaurant gives diners a way to encounter the dish wherever there is a cook able to prepare it and a supplier able to provide the ingredients. A culinary school can teach cooks outside the households where the practice developed. This is a different route for passing on a tradition from learning to make borscht at a family gathering. It also creates demand for ingredients. UNESCO records proposed work on freshwater prawn farming and on organic culinary herbs, connecting the spread of the soup to the resources on which cooks depend.

What keeps a food tradition in use

These examples show three ways a tradition can continue. A market can pay for the skills involved in producing a food, as with attiéké. A gathering gives people a reason to prepare a dish and a place to teach it, as with borscht. Conservation can protect the ingredients that fishers and cooks rely on, as with ceviche. UNESCO’s Convention treats heritage as something communities pass on and adapt to their surroundings. The map shows where those practices can be observed and gives a reason to look at the people, work and resources behind each dish. UNESCO Convention, Article 2
