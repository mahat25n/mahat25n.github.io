A Taste of Earth

A meal brings several kinds of work to the same table. Farmers and fishers supply ingredients, cooks turn them into familiar dishes, and families teach the skills needed to make them again. I chose five foods from UNESCO?s Intangible Cultural Heritage records to show how those connections differ from place to place.

How to read the animation

The opening Mollweide equal-area map shows all five stops. The camera then flies between them on an orthographic globe. Each ring highlights a selected location associated with a food tradition. The pins serve as reference points for practices that extend across wider areas. The blue lights come from NASA?s 2016 Black Marble imagery.

The economics of keeping a food tradition alive

Mexico ? Maize tortillas

Mexico?s inscription covers a food system, including cultivation, preparation and ritual. The milpa is a farming system in which maize can grow alongside other crops. Cooks treat maize with an alkaline solution before grinding it into dough, a process called nixtamalization. The tortilla depends on knowledge held on both sides of the kitchen door. UNESCO describes collectives of women cooks and other practitioners in Michoac?n who pass on those skills. UNESCO, 2010

Peru ? Ceviche

Ceviche connects artisanal fishers, farming families and cooks in cevicher?as. The catch and locally grown ingredients shape regional versions of the dish. Eating ceviche during the Feast of Saint Peter, patron saint of artisanal fishers, connects a working livelihood to a public celebration. The UNESCO record also describes how communities work to protect fish species they use, linking the future of the dish to the resource on which fishers and cooks depend. UNESCO, 2023

C?te d?Ivoire ? Atti?k?

Processing turns cassava into a food sold for everyday meals and served at ceremonies. Among C?te d?Ivoire?s lagoon communities, UNESCO describes women preparing, preserving and selling atti?k?, with men involved in growing, harvesting and grinding cassava. The skills women pass on through observation and practice give them a way to earn income from a household staple. UNESCO inscribed the production skills. UNESCO, 2024

Ukraine ? Borscht

Children learn to make borscht by cooking with their families. Serving it to guests and at weddings gives people reasons to keep making it and passing it on. War and displacement interrupt that learning by separating families and limiting access to gardens and local vegetables. The risk to those everyday settings helps explain why UNESCO placed the culture of Ukrainian borscht cooking on its Urgent Safeguarding List. UNESCO, 2022

Thailand ? Tomyum Kung

Freshwater prawns and locally available herbs shaped this soup in the riverside communities of Thailand?s Central Plains. Families teach how to choose and combine the ingredients. Restaurants and culinary schools also carry that knowledge to cooks and diners outside the communities where it developed. The dish shows how a food rooted in one region can travel through restaurant markets and training. UNESCO, 2024
